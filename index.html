Spotting the Bait

How to identify suspicious emails, fake login pages, and fraudulent websites.

Suspicious Emails

  • Check the Sender: Look closely at the email address. Is it slightly misspelled? (e.g., support@amozon.com instead of amazon.com)
  • Urgent Tone: Phishing emails often create a false sense of urgency (e.g., "Act immediately," "Account suspended").
  • Generic Greetings: Using "Dear Customer" instead of your actual name.

Fake Login Pages & URLs

  • Inspect the URL: Hover over links without clicking to see where they lead.
  • Missing HTTPS: Look for the padlock icon in the browser, though attackers can use HTTPS too. Check the domain carefully.
  • Poor Design: Look for low-resolution logos, spelling mistakes, or strange layouts.

The Art of Manipulation

Common social engineering techniques cybercriminals use to trick you.

Spear Phishing

Unlike generic phishing, spear phishing is highly targeted. Attackers research you (using social media) to craft a customized email that looks like it's from someone you know, like your boss or a colleague.

Whaling

A specific form of spear phishing targeting high-profile individuals like CEOs or CFOs. The goal is often to authorize large wire transfers or steal highly sensitive company data.

Vishing & Smishing

Vishing (Voice Phishing): Fraudulent phone calls claiming to be from your bank or tech support.
Smishing (SMS Phishing): Phishing attempts sent via text message, often containing malicious links.

Real-world Consequences

Examine realistic case studies to understand the impact of phishing.

Case Study A: The Fake Invoice

The Attack

An employee in accounts payable received an email from their "vendor" containing an updated invoice. The email looked legitimate, mimicking the vendor's signature and logo.

The Mistake

The employee didn't notice the sender's email domain was slightly altered (e.g., vendor-inc.com instead of vendor.com). They downloaded the attached PDF invoice.

The Impact

The PDF contained malware that deployed ransomware across the corporate network, paralyzing operations for a week.

Case Study B: The Urgent CEO Request

The Attack

A junior finance manager received an email from the "CEO" while the CEO was allegedly traveling. The email urgently requested a $50,000 wire transfer for a "confidential acquisition."

The Mistake

Feeling pressured by the "CEO's" authority and the urgency of the request, the manager bypassed standard verification procedures and authorized the transfer.

The Impact

The funds were sent to an offshore account controlled by attackers and were unrecoverable. This is a classic example of Business Email Compromise (BEC).

Stay Secure

Practical security tips to protect yourself and your organization.

01

Think Before You Click

Never click on links or download attachments in unsolicited emails. Always verify the source first.

02

Enable MFA

Use Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) on all accounts. Even if attackers steal your password, they can't access your account without the second factor.

03

Verify via Alternate Channels

If you receive a suspicious request for money or sensitive data, verify it by calling the sender using a trusted phone number (not the one in the email).

04

Keep Software Updated

Regularly update your operating system, browser, and antivirus software to patch security vulnerabilities.
